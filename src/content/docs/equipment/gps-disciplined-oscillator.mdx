The GPSDO (GPS Disciplined Oscillator) is the frequency reference heart of your entire RF system. It provides an ultra-stable 10 MHz reference signal that all other equipment (BUC, LNB, modems) lock to for frequency accuracy. Think of it as the "master clock" that ensures all your transmit and receive frequencies are precisely where they should be. Without a stable reference, your signals would drift off-frequency and become unusable.

The GPSDO works by disciplining (controlling) a high-quality oven-controlled crystal oscillator (OCXO) using GPS satellite timing signals. GPS satellites carry atomic clocks accurate to nanoseconds, and by comparing the local oscillator to GPS time, the GPSDO achieves frequency accuracy better than 1 part per billion - far beyond what any free-running crystal could achieve.

## Where to find it

The GPSDO is on the **GPS Timing** tab of Mission Control. The tab is split into cards: Lock & Power Status, GNSS Constellation, OCXO Oven Control, 10 MHz Distribution, Reference Quality Metrics and Holdover Performance. See [GPS Timing](/mission-control/gps-timing/) and [Timing and the GPSDO](/systems/timing-and-gpsdo/).

## Controls

| Control | What it does | Typical value |
|---|---|---|
| Power | Powers the GPSDO. It then begins a warm-up of about 10 minutes while the oven heats the crystal to about 70 degrees C. Accuracy is poor and improves during warm-up. | On |
| GNSS Input | Enables or disables GPS reception. With it off the unit enters holdover and runs on the oven oscillator alone, drifting slowly. | On |

Two things put the unit in holdover. Switching GNSS Input off is one. The other is a GNSS outage staged by the scenario while the switch stays on: GNSS Signal reads NO SIGNAL, Satellites Tracked drops to 0, Holdover reads ACTIVE, and Lock Status still reads LOCKED. When the signal returns, the unit re-locks and the holdover error resets to zero.

## How it works

### What is GPS disciplining and why does it matter?

A crystal oscillator, even a high-quality oven-controlled one, will drift over time due to aging, temperature changes, and other factors. Free-running accuracy might be 1–10 ppm (parts per million), which translates to kilohertz of error at gigahertz frequencies - far too much for satellite communications.

GPS satellites broadcast signals from atomic clocks accurate to ~1 part per trillion. The GPSDO receives these GPS signals, measures the difference between the local oscillator and GPS time, and generates a correction signal that "disciplines" (adjusts) the oscillator to match GPS frequency. This closed-loop control achieves accuracy of about 2 ×10⁻¹² - around a million times more accurate than the free-running crystal.

The result: your 10 MHz reference is locked to atomic time, ensuring all downstream equipment (BUC, LNB, modems) has the same frequency reference as the rest of the world's communications infrastructure. The other side of that is that every LO locked to the GPSDO carries the GPSDO's error, whatever it is: in holdover, in warmup, or when the GNSS timing it trusts is false (see *GNSS spoofing* below).

### Warmup process and thermal stability

Crystal oscillators are temperature-sensitive - their frequency changes with temperature. The OCXO solves this by enclosing the crystal in an electronically-controlled oven that maintains constant temperature (~70°C) regardless of ambient conditions.

When you first power on the GPSDO, the oven is cold (room temperature, ~25°C). It takes approximately 10 minutes to heat up to operating temperature. During this warmup period:

*   The Warmup is counting down, showing warmup in progress
*   The WARMUP display counts down minutes:seconds remaining
*   Frequency accuracy is poor (about 10 parts per billion at first) and gradually improves
*   The Lock Status reads UNLOCKED - the unit cannot achieve GPS lock while warming up
*   Temperature reading climbs from ~25°C toward 70°C

Once warmup completes (Warmup shows "READY", Warmup reads READY), the oven has reached thermal equilibrium and frequency stability is achieved. Only then can the GPS disciplining loop lock and provide the specified accuracy.

**Important:** Do not use the GPSDO as a reference during warmup. Wait until Warmup reads READY and Lock Status is LOCKED before connecting downstream equipment or transmitting.

### Holdover mode - maintaining accuracy when GPS is lost

Holdover is the ability to maintain frequency accuracy for some time after losing GPS signal. When the GNSS Input is disabled or GPS reception is lost (antenna disconnected, jammed, indoors, etc.), the GPSDO enters holdover mode:

*   The GNSS Signal reads NO SIGNAL (no GPS signals)
*   The GPSDO continues operating using only the OCXO
*   The frequency settles at a small constant offset, about 4.6 ×10⁻¹⁰ (Frequency Accuracy reads about 46 ×10⁻¹¹), plus any configured crystal ageing
*   That offset builds up a time error of ~1.67 μs per hour
*   Specification: \<40 μs drift over 24 hours

During holdover, the 10 MHz frequency remains very stable (the OCXO is still temperature-controlled), but there's no GPS correction, so the small offset stays uncorrected and the time error keeps growing. The offset also reaches every LO locked to the GPSDO: at 7 GHz, 4.6 ×10⁻¹⁰ is about 3 Hz, harmless for a carrier but not for a timing application. This is acceptable for short-term GPS outages (minutes to hours) but not for extended operation.

### GNSS spoofing

A GPSDO trusts the GNSS time it receives. A spoofer that walks that time away from the truth at X μs per second steers the disciplined frequency off by X parts per million for as long as the GPSDO keeps trusting GNSS, and every LO locked to it moves with it: 1 μs/s is 7 kHz at 7 GHz. The Lock Status can stay LOCKED the whole time; the clues are a Frequency Accuracy far above its usual 0.2 ×10⁻¹¹ and a UTC reading that keeps growing.

The HOLDOVER readout shows accumulated error. When it exceeds 30 μs, an alarm is raised. At 40 μs (24 hours), the holdover specification is exceeded and frequency accuracy may no longer meet requirements for satellite communications.

**Best practice:** Holdover is an emergency backup, not a normal operating mode. If GPS is lost, investigate and restore GPS reception as soon as possible. Do not rely on holdover for more than a few hours.

### Understanding Allan deviation (stability metric)

Allan deviation is a statistical measure of frequency stability over time. Unlike simple "accuracy" (how close to the correct frequency), stability describes how much the frequency varies from moment to moment. It's measured at specific time intervals (in this case, 1 second).

For example, an Allan deviation of 1 ×10⁻¹¹ at 1 second means that if you measure the frequency twice, one second apart, the difference will typically be 1 part in 100 billion. Lower Allan deviation means more stable, predictable frequency.

Why it matters: High-order modulation schemes and precise Doppler tracking require not just accurate average frequency, but also low short-term variations. A frequency source with good long-term accuracy but poor short-term stability will still degrade signal quality. The GPSDO's excellent Allan deviation (1 ×10⁻¹¹) ensures both accuracy and stability.

### Phase noise and its impact

Phase noise represents random phase fluctuations in the oscillator output. These fluctuations appear as noise sidebands around the reference frequency and propagate through all equipment locked to the reference (BUC, LNB, modems). High phase noise degrades:

*   Receiver sensitivity (noise floor increases)
*   Modulation quality (phase errors in digital modulation)
*   Adjacent channel interference (spectral spreading)
*   Phase-locked loop performance (harder to maintain lock)

The GPSDO's phase noise specification (-127 dBc/Hz at 10 Hz offset once warmed up) is excellent and ensures that the reference contributes negligible phase noise to the overall system. For comparison, a typical free-running crystal might have -90 to -100 dBc/Hz, and a low-cost oscillator might be -70 to -80 dBc/Hz.

## Status and alarms

The system will raise alarms for several conditions:

*   **GPSDO not locked** - Warmup is complete but GPS lock has not been achieved. Check GNSS Input is on, antenna is connected, and GPS reception is adequate (4+ satellites). Verify no RF interference or jamming.
*   **GNSS signal lost** - GPS reception has failed. Check antenna cable connection, verify antenna has clear sky view (not obstructed by buildings, trees, or indoors), and ensure GNSS Input is on. If indoors, you may need an external antenna with cable to a window or roof.
*   **GPSDO in holdover (X μs error)** - GPS is lost and the unit is operating in holdover mode. Accuracy is degrading. Shows accumulated holdover error. Restore GPS reception to exit holdover and re-lock.
*   **GPSDO holdover approaching limit (>30 μs)** - Holdover error exceeds 30 μs. The 24-hour specification (40 μs) is approaching. Frequency accuracy is marginal. GPS must be restored immediately or frequency reference will become unreliable.
*   **GPSDO oven temperature out of range (X °C)** - Oven temperature is outside the normal 65–75°C range. This indicates oven control malfunction. Frequency stability is compromised. Power cycle the unit; if alarm persists, hardware service is required.
*   **GPSDO self-test failed** - Internal diagnostics detected a fault. The reference may not be trustworthy. Power cycle and retry. If failure persists, contact technical support - the unit may require calibration or repair.

## Troubleshooting

1.  **Lock Status is UNLOCKED, Warmup is counting down** - Unit is still warming up. This is normal on power-on. Wait 10 minutes for warmup to complete (Warmup shows "READY", Warmup reads READY). Then GPS lock will be attempted.
2.  **Lock Status is UNLOCKED, Warmup reads READY, GNSS Signal is NO SIGNAL** - Warmup complete but no GPS signal. Check GNSS Input is on (up position). Verify GPS antenna is connected and has clear sky view. If indoors, GPS reception is usually impossible - move antenna to window or outside.
3.  **Lock Status is ACQUIRING** - GPS signals present, attempting to lock. This can take 30 seconds to several minutes depending on satellite geometry and signal strength. Wait for LOCK status to turn green. If stuck in acquiring for >5 minutes, power cycle the GPSDO.
4.  **GNSS Signal is WEAK (low satellite count)** - Fewer than 4 satellites tracked. GPS lock is marginal or impossible. Improve antenna location for better sky visibility. Ensure antenna has unobstructed view of horizon in all directions. Nearby buildings or terrain can block satellites.
5.  **HOLDOVER error is increasing** - GPS reception was lost and the unit is in holdover mode. Check GNSS Input (should be up/enabled). Inspect antenna cable for damage or disconnection. Verify antenna has not been moved or blocked. Once GPS is restored, holdover error will reset to 0 and the unit will re-lock. If GNSS Input is already up and Satellites Tracked reads 0, the outage is external. Wait for the signal and log the start and end times.
6.  **Frequency accuracy is poor despite a LOCKED status** - A locked GPSDO reads about 0.2 ×10⁻¹¹. If Lock Status is LOCKED but the accuracy is far higher and the UTC reading keeps growing, the GPSDO is faithfully following GNSS timing that is wrong: suspect spoofing (see *GNSS spoofing* above), and remember that every LO locked to the reference is moving too. Report it before trusting the reference.
7.  **Downstream equipment (BUC, LNB) won't lock to reference** - Verify GPSDO is powered, warmed up (Warmup reads READY), and locked (Lock Status is LOCKED). Check 10 MHz output cables are connected and not damaged. Confirm OUTPUTS readout shows the expected number of active outputs. Verify downstream equipment is powered and configured to lock to external reference.

## Advanced

### How GPS disciplining works (PLL control)

The GPS disciplining circuit is a phase-locked loop (PLL) that compares the local 10 MHz oscillator to GPS time signals. GPS satellites transmit a precise 1PPS (one pulse per second) timing signal synchronized to atomic clocks. The GPSDO's receiver generates a local 1PPS derived from the 10 MHz oscillator (by dividing 10,000,000 Hz by 10,000,000 to get 1 Hz).

The PLL compares the phase (timing) of these two 1PPS signals:

*   If the local 1PPS arrives early compared to GPS 1PPS, the oscillator is running fast
*   If the local 1PPS arrives late, the oscillator is running slow

The PLL generates a control voltage that adjusts the oscillator's frequency to bring the phases into alignment. Over time (minutes to hours), this feedback loop "disciplines" the oscillator to match GPS frequency exactly. The loop has a long time constant (slow response) because it's averaging over many measurements to filter out noise and short-term variations.

Once locked, the PLL continuously monitors and corrects for any drift, aging, or temperature effects, keeping the oscillator accurate to GPS even as conditions change.

### Why 10 MHz and not other frequencies?

10 MHz is a standard reference frequency in RF and test equipment for several reasons:

*   **Convenient scaling:** It's easy to multiply (for microwave frequencies) or divide (for timing and digital clocks). Many frequencies are simple integer ratios of 10 MHz.
*   **Crystal availability:** High-quality quartz crystals at 10 MHz are mature, well-characterized, and readily available from multiple manufacturers.
*   **Cable distribution:** 10 MHz is low enough that cable losses are minimal and phase shifts are manageable, yet high enough to carry timing information with good resolution.
*   **Industry standardization:** Test equipment, synthesizers, and communication systems have standardized on 10 MHz for decades, creating an ecosystem of compatible gear.

Your BUC, LNB, and modems all have internal PLLs that lock to this 10 MHz reference and multiply it up to the gigahertz frequencies used for satellite communications, maintaining the same fractional accuracy as the reference.

## Final notes

The GPSDO is arguably the most critical single component in a professional satellite ground station. Without a stable, accurate frequency reference, every downstream system suffers: transmitted signals are off-frequency (missing the transponder or interfering with others), received signals cannot be properly tuned, and modulation quality degrades.

Best practices for GPSDO operation:

*   Install GPS antenna with clear view of the sky (ideally rooftop, minimum window location)
*   Use quality low-loss coaxial cable for GPS antenna (LMR-400 or better, keep runs short)
*   Allow full 10-minute warmup after power-on before using the reference
*   Verify Lock Status is LOCKED before transmitting or operating other equipment
*   Monitor satellite count - if it drops below 6, investigate antenna positioning
*   Never operate in holdover mode for more than a few hours - it's emergency backup only
*   Keep the GPSDO powered continuously (24/7 operation) to avoid repeated warmup cycles
*   Check frequency accuracy and phase noise periodically to verify performance

If your GPSDO fails, the entire ground station becomes inoperable. Consider redundancy (dual GPSDOs with automatic failover) for critical operations, or maintain a spare unit for rapid replacement.

Remember: GPS is a broadcast service that can be affected by solar weather, satellite maintenance, or RF interference. Monitor GNSS reception health and have a plan for GPS outages. In professional installations, consider a cesium or rubidium atomic clock as a backup reference with extended holdover capability (weeks to months instead of hours).
