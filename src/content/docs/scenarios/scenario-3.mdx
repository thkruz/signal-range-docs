## Background Concepts

### Ice Protection and Feed Heaters

Satellite ground station antennas use waveguide assemblies to carry RF signals between the feed horn and the electronics. These waveguides are precision-machined metal tubes where the internal dimensions are critical to RF performance.

**Why Ice is Dangerous:**

Ice accumulation on the feed assembly causes several problems:

1. **Signal Attenuation**: Ice absorbs RF energy, particularly at higher frequencies. Even a thin layer can add 1-2 dB of loss.

2. **Impedance Mismatch**: Ice changes the electrical characteristics of the feed horn, causing reflections that reduce power transfer efficiency.

3. **Mechanical Damage**: Ice adds weight to the feed assembly. Combined with wind, this creates stress on mounting hardware and can physically damage the waveguide or feed horn.

4. **Water Ingress**: As ice melts and refreezes, it can work its way into waveguide joints. Water inside a waveguide causes severe signal degradation and can permanently damage components.

**How Feed Heaters Work:**

Feed heaters are resistive heating elements wrapped around the waveguide and feed horn assembly. When activated, they maintain the metal temperature above freezing, preventing ice formation. Most systems draw 200-500 watts and can keep the feed clear in temperatures down to -30°C.

<Aside type="tip">
**Operational Practice**

Professional operators enable feed heaters proactively based on weather forecasts, not reactively after ice has formed. Removing existing ice is much harder than preventing it.
</Aside>

### Geographic Diversity in Satellite Operations

Commercial satellite operators maintain multiple ground stations for several critical reasons:

**Weather Resilience:**

Weather systems are geographically limited. A blizzard affecting Vermont won't simultaneously impact a site 150 miles away in Maine. By maintaining diverse sites, operators can route traffic around weather events.

**The 100-Mile Rule:**

Industry experience shows that sites separated by at least 100 miles rarely experience simultaneous severe weather. NATS maintains Vermont and Maine stations specifically for this diversity.

**Equipment Redundancy:**

Hardware failures happen. A backup site allows continued operations during repairs or maintenance at the primary facility.

**Network Optimization:**

Sometimes the backup site actually has better link conditions than the primary. Operators can shift traffic to optimize performance, not just recover from failures.

### GPSDO: The Heartbeat of RF Systems

Every piece of RF equipment in a ground station depends on a stable frequency reference. The GPS Disciplined Oscillator (GPSDO) provides this reference by locking a precision oscillator to GPS timing signals.

**Why Timing Matters:**

1. **Carrier Frequency Accuracy**: The modem must transmit and receive on exact frequencies. A frequency error of even a few Hertz can cause the satellite to reject your signal or cause interference to adjacent channels.

2. **Symbol Timing**: Digital modulation requires precise timing to sample the signal at exactly the right moments. Timing drift causes bit errors.

3. **Phase Coherence**: Many modern modulation schemes (like QPSK) encode information in phase. Without stable phase reference, the receiver can't decode the signal.

**The Reference Chain:**

```
GPS Satellites → GPSDO → 10 MHz Reference → LNB, BUC, Modem
```

Every component in the RF chain locks to the GPSDO's 10 MHz output. If the GPSDO isn't locked, nothing else will work correctly.

<Aside type="caution">
**Critical Sequence**

Always verify GPSDO lock before configuring any RF equipment. Configuring an LNB or modem without stable reference will result in incorrect frequencies and failed signal acquisition.
</Aside>

### LNB Thermal Stabilization

The Low Noise Block (LNB) converts high-frequency RF signals from the satellite to lower intermediate frequencies (IF) for processing. Its performance is highly temperature-dependent.

**Why Temperature Matters:**

1. **Noise Figure**: The LNB's noise figure—how much noise it adds to the signal—varies with temperature. Cold LNBs have higher noise figures, degrading C/N ratio.

2. **Local Oscillator Drift**: The LNB's internal oscillator frequency shifts as temperature changes. This causes the output IF frequency to drift, potentially moving the signal outside the modem's acquisition window.

3. **Gain Variation**: LNB gain changes with temperature. Cold LNBs may have lower gain, reducing signal levels at the modem.

**Stabilization Time:**

A cold LNB (at ambient winter temperature) typically needs 2-4 minutes to reach stable operating temperature after power-on. The internal components must warm up and reach thermal equilibrium.

**What "Thermally Stable" Means:**

- Noise temperature has settled to its rated value
- LO frequency drift is within specification
- Gain is consistent and predictable

### Make-Before-Break Handovers

Traffic handovers between ground stations follow a critical principle: **never break the existing path until the new path is proven.**

**The Sequence:**

1. **Make**: Establish the new path completely. Point the backup antenna, configure the receive chain, verify carrier lock, configure the transmit chain, verify uplink.

2. **Verify**: Confirm the new path meets all operational thresholds. C/N ratio, bit error rate, packet throughput—everything must be within specification.

3. **Break**: Only after verification, transfer traffic from the old path to the new one.

**Why This Matters:**

If you disable the primary site before the backup is ready, customers experience an outage. Even a few seconds of packet loss can trigger alarms, violate SLAs, and damage customer relationships.

**The Professional Standard:**

Well-executed handovers achieve less than 0.1% packet loss—essentially imperceptible to end users. This requires both paths to be fully operational simultaneously during the transition.

### Link Budget and Weather Margin

Every satellite link has a "link budget"—an accounting of all gains and losses between transmitter and receiver. The difference between the received signal power and the minimum required power is the **link margin**.

**Typical C-band Link Budget:**

| Factor | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Clear-sky margin | 12 dB |
| Rain/snow attenuation | 4-6 dB |
| Antenna accumulation | 2-3 dB |
| **Net margin in storm** | **3-5 dB** |

**The Decision Point:**

When projected margin drops below 6 dB, professional operators initiate handover procedures. This provides buffer for:
- Forecast uncertainty
- Unexpected additional degradation
- Time needed to complete handover

**Why C-band Handles Weather Better:**

Lower frequencies experience less atmospheric attenuation:
- C-band (4-6 GHz): 4-6 dB in heavy rain
- Ku-band (12-18 GHz): 10-15 dB in heavy rain
- Ka-band (26-40 GHz): 15-25 dB in heavy rain

This is why critical services (maritime, aviation, military) often use C-band despite its larger antenna requirements.

## Mission Phases

<Steps>

1. **Activate Weather Protection**

   Enable the VT-01 feed heater before the storm arrives. Ice accumulation on the waveguide degrades signal quality and can damage equipment.

2. **Access Backup Site Controls**

   Use the asset menu to switch to ME-02. Vermont continues serving traffic while you configure Maine.

3. **Verify Reference Timing**

   Confirm the ME-02 GPSDO is locked before configuring any RF equipment. Without stable reference, frequency settings will be incorrect.

4. **Acquire TIDEMARK-1 from Maine**

   Point the Maine antenna to TIDEMARK-1 using program-track mode.

5. **Power Up Receive Chain**

   Power on the LNB and configure it to match Vermont's settings. Wait for thermal stabilization before proceeding.

6. **Configure Receiver Modem**

   Set the receiver modem parameters to match the TIDEMARK-1 carrier exactly.

7. **Confirm Signal Acquisition**

   Verify the receiver achieves carrier lock with C/N ratio ≥ 10 dB.

8. **Enable ME-02 Transmitter**

   Configure and enable the transmitter to match VT-01's uplink settings.

9. **Execute Traffic Handover**

   Transfer active traffic from VT-01 to ME-02. Monitor both sites during transition.

10. **Protect Vermont Antenna**

    Stow the Vermont antenna to protect it from wind loading and ice accumulation during the storm.

</Steps>

## Common Mistakes

*   ❌ **Skipping feed heater**: Ice damage is expensive and preventable
*   ❌ **Configuring RF before GPSDO lock**: All frequencies will be wrong
*   ❌ **Rushing thermal stabilization**: Unstable LNB causes acquisition failures
*   ❌ **Forgetting transmitter configuration**: Can't hand over without uplink capability
*   ❌ **Breaking before making**: Never disable primary until backup is verified
