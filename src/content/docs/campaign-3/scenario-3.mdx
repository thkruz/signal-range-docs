## Reading a Loss

When a link comes in worse than predicted, the *shape* of the loss tells you
where to look before you touch anything:

| What you observe | What it usually means |
|------------------|----------------------|
| Loss varies with pointing | Antenna aim, tracking mode, or a beam too narrow |
| Loss varies with time / fades in and out | Multipath, obstruction, scintillation |
| Loss appears only at low elevation | Trees, terrain, atmosphere |
| **Large, constant loss with everything else nominal** | Something systematic in the RF chain |

That last row is the interesting one. A big flat number that doesn't care
about geometry, doesn't fluctuate, and doesn't improve at high elevation is
not a propagation problem. It's a configuration problem.

---

## Circular Polarization Reference

The crossed yagi has a **switchable feed**: it can be configured for
right-hand or left-hand circular polarization. That switch is in the RADIO
section of the console, marked **POL**.

| Situation | Loss |
|-----------|------|
| Feed handedness matches the signal | ~0.5 dB |
| Feed handedness is opposite the signal | **~18 dB** |

<Aside type="caution">
Eighteen decibels is a factor of sixty in power. A cross-polarized signal is
still plainly visible on the waterfall — it just refuses to lock, forever, no
matter how well you point or tune. Seeing a signal and being able to use it
are different privileges.
</Aside>

### One More Thing About Handedness

A circularly polarized signal that **reflects** off something — a metal roof,
a car, a wet street — comes back with its handedness **reversed**. RHCP goes
in, LHCP comes out. That's worth remembering the first time a signal is
strong in a direction where there's nothing but a building.

## End of Briefing

Riley did not sabotage the rig. Riley made an honest improvement and
introduced one wrong assumption while doing it, which is precisely how this
happens to real operators on real equipment.

The lesson isn't "check the polarization." The lesson is that **the size and
shape of a loss tells you which subsystem to suspect** — and that a signal you
can see is not necessarily a signal you can use.

Sixty-three degrees. Don't waste it.
